  • Taylor expansion: φ(t) ≈ 1 + iμt − (σ² + μ²)·t²/2 + O(t³). A small t shows only the mean and the variance. Only a larger t shows the shape, for example skewness and kurtosis.

Contribution of each knot to the loss

N · wk · err(tk). The sum of all bars = SIGReg. The gray curve is the shape of the window e−t²/2.

  • Vertical axis: the top = max(1, highest bar) × 1.1. So when the top is 1.1, every bar is less than 1.
  • Gray curve: it only shows how the weight decreases as t increases. It is scaled to the panel height and does not use the vertical scale of the bars.
  • Why the bars go up and then down: each bar = err(t) × weight. err(t) increases with t (at t = 0 all points are at (1, 0), so err is always 0). The weight wk = trapezoid coefficient (Δt at the two ends, 2Δt in the middle) × e−t²/2 decreases with t (at t = 3 it is only about 0.011). The product is high in the middle and low at the two sides.
  • A perfect N(0,1) also has this shape: with only N samples, the centroid moves randomly. The expected noise is E[N·err(t)] = 1 − e−t², so a bar from pure noise ≈ 2Δt · e−t²/2 · (1 − e−t²). The maximum is at t = √ln3 ≈ 1.05, and this value does not depend on N.
  • So: the position of the peak does not always mean that there is a problem near t ≈ 1. The bar heights depend on the samples. When you click "Resample", the heights change a lot, but the shape stays almost the same. For N(0,1), the sum of all bars (SIGReg) is about 1.0 on average.
  • Compare the distributions: Collapsed, Too wide, and Shifted have large differences at small and medium t. Their peak is on the left, and their bars are very high. For Uniform, Bimodal, and Laplace, the differences appear only at larger t, so the peak moves to the right. But the window is small on the right, so these differences have much less weight in the loss.
  • What the window does: SIGReg mainly penalizes low-order errors (mean and variance). It penalizes higher-order differences in shape less, because the estimate is noisier at large t.

How to read this page

  • Small t: all points are close to angle 0, and the centroid is near (1, 0).
  • Larger t: the points spread around the circle, and the centroid moves toward the center. For N(0,1), the centroid moves in at exactly the rate e−t²/2.
  • Collapsed: the points do not spread, so the centroid stays near (1, 0). It is larger than the target.
  • Too wide: the points spread too fast, so the centroid moves in too fast.
  • Shifted: the whole point cloud turns by t·μ, so Im is not 0.
  • Bimodal / Uniform / Laplace: the mean and the variance are both correct. A small t cannot show the difference. Only a larger t finds it.

err(t) = (Re φ̂ − e−t²/2)² + Im φ̂², the squared distance from the centroid to the target point.

The same steps in module.py:

x_t = (proj @ A)[..., None] * t
err = (cos.mean − phi)² + sin.mean²
stat = (err @ weights) * N

In real training, A has 1024 random directions. The model does this 1-D test once for each direction, then takes the average. By the Cramér–Wold theorem, all 1-D projections are N(0,1) ⇔ the full distribution is an isotropic Gaussian.

Why the characteristic function

  • Differentiable: it uses only cos and sin, so gradients flow back directly.
  • Bounded: every point is on the unit circle, so one outlier changes the result by at most 1/N. If you match higher moments directly (for example x⁴), one outlier can have a very large effect.
  • No bins: unlike a histogram, there is no bin width to tune.
  • Fully defines the distribution: two distributions have the same characteristic function ⇔ they are the same distribution.

In one sentence: the characteristic function is the centroid of the samples after you wrap them around the circle at speed t. SIGReg requires that, at every t ∈ [0, 3], this centroid is at the centroid of a standard Gaussian, (e−t²/2, 0).
